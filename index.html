05 — SEPARATING MIX FROM MARGIN

Central’s weakness survives a fairer product comparison

Regions sell different combinations of products. To compare them on a common basis, assign every region the same sales shares across the 17 sub-categories, while retaining each region’s observed margin in each sub-category.

Central remains last at 6.9%. South rises from 11.9% to 14.3% and moves ahead of East under the common mix. The ranking is sensitive to what each region sells, but Central’s problem persists.

This is an accounting comparison, not a forecast of what would happen if the assortment changed. It controls for broad sub-category sales shares only. Product models, discount levels, customer mix, shipping, timing and quantities can still differ within a sub-category.

How the common-mix comparison works

For each sub-category, divide its sales summed across the four regions by total regional sales. Multiply that common weight by each region’s sub-category margin (profit ÷ sales), then sum across all 17 groups. All margins in this extension use the underlying displayed dollar totals, rather than rounded percentage fields. A percentage-point change is the difference between two percentages.

Binders, Appliances and Furnishings contribute −5.78 percentage points together. That is about 84% of the net within-sub-category component; better results in Phones and Copiers offset part of the weakness elsewhere. Binders are especially worth examining: the national total is profitable, but Central’s total is negative.

This identifies where to investigate, not who or what is responsible. Possible explanations include deeper discounting, different items within the same group, unfavorable costs or customer mix. The aggregate data cannot distinguish them.

Decomposition formula and benchmark choice

Let w be a sub-category’s share of regional sales and m its profit-to-sales margin. “C” is Central and “O” combines West, East and South. Mix = Σ(wC − wO)mO; within = ΣwC(mC − mO). Their sum equals Central’s margin minus the others’ margin. This uses the other regions as the reference; a different reference can change the split. Dollar-level rounding causes small differences from headline KPIs.

07 — LOSSES HIDDEN BY AGGREGATION

A profitable national category can contain an unprofitable region

Across the four regions, 14 sub-category–region groups have negative aggregate profit totaling $36,971. Looking only at nationally negative sub-categories identifies $22,387. Regional gains offset $14,584 of losses in the national view, making local problems less visible.

These figures are net losses within groups, not the sum of all losing transactions. Profitable transactions inside each group may offset additional losses. Neither figure is automatically recoverable profit.

What would a targeted intervention need to deliver?

Illustrative arithmetic, not a forecast. Hold sales constant and assume a selected share of this view’s net negative group profit is eliminated. Subtract an incremental intervention cost.

Net change = selected loss pool × assumed recovery share − intervention cost. Demand, cross-selling and cost avoidability are unknown; real results can be worse, including beyond this range. Do not add this loss pool to discount-tier losses: they overlap.

08 — QUANTIFYING DISCOUNT EXPOSURE

A small share of records offsets a substantial share of profit

In the two negative-margin source tiers, 1,393 records—13.9% of the dataset—sum to $135,376 in net losses. They offset 32.1% of the $421,773 earned in the other two tiers. The resulting total is $286,397.

The deepest tier accounts for $99,559 of these losses; Central contributes $40,793, or 41.0%, of that tier’s net loss. That makes it a sensible place to audit exceptions. It does not establish a universal safe discount threshold, and the source labels do not resolve how exactly 40% was classified.

The next question is incremental profit: would customers still buy with a smaller discount, and at what quantity? Simply removing a discount assumes away the demand response. The current aggregates cannot estimate that response, inventory holding costs or the value of clearing stock.

Next empirical layer: compare like with like

Obtain transaction records with order and line identifiers, date, product ID, region, customer segment, quantity, discount, sales and profit, plus the definitions of cost and returns. Separate unique orders from line items and verify dates, duplicates, missing values and returns before fitting a model.

Within products that appear at multiple discount levels, compare profit and quantities within similar time periods and regions. Report sample sizes and overlap: products observed at only one discount cannot support a within-product discount comparison. A model with product and time controls can describe conditional associations, but selective discounting still prevents a causal interpretation.

Test a policy, not just a correlation

For eligible products and customers, predefine a smaller-discount policy and compare it with current practice through a randomized pilot where feasible. Randomize at a level that limits spillovers, such as store or customer group. Use incremental contribution profit per eligible customer or store over the test window as the primary outcome, including non-purchases, returns and implementation cost. Set guardrails for conversion, volume, inventory clearance and repeat purchase. Choose test length and sample size from expected variability and a business-relevant minimum effect before launch.

Conclusion for management: the strongest next move is a targeted review of local product economics. Central’s weaker margin is not explained by broad product mix alone, and company-wide totals hide several regional losses. Use these comparisons to prioritize investigations, then measure whether a specific policy improves profit without unacceptable losses in demand or service.
DATA & REPRODUCIBILITY

This extension recalculates published aggregates from the original dashboard, retrieved September 16, 2026. It does not recreate the original transaction analysis. The public repository contained only HTML; its linked analysis_queries.sql returned 404.

“Profit” means the dataset’s recorded field, not verified net income or contribution margin. Counts are labeled source records because unique orders and line items cannot be distinguished from the aggregates. The original claims of “real orders” and losses “on every sale” are not substantiated by the available files.

Rounded totals differ by a few dollars across views. New margin comparisons use sums of displayed sub-category dollars consistently. Discount margins and record counts are retained as published; bucket boundaries, average-discount weighting and period coverage require the original source. No p-values, demand elasticity estimates or causal effects can be recovered from these summaries.
